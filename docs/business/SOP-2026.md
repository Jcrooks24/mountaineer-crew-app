## 3 · Sales Pipeline & Conversion

This section covers direct-to-Mountaineer leads. Jobs that originate through M1 are pre-sold and do not enter this pipeline; they proceed straight to scheduling (§5) with M1's administrator confirming the booking.

### 3.1 The pipeline

- Intake & documentation. A lead arrives by call, email, Yelp, LSA, website form, or referral. Document it in SmartMoving immediately — pin the call notes and open a task. Large-ticket jobs (over $3,000) are flagged to Jonas or Jacob the same day.
- Qualify & capture. Work the sales script (§3.3). Gather the required intake before the call ends — contact information, addresses, dates, service type, access details, and inventory.
- Walkthrough decision. Certain jobs merit a free in-person walkthrough before an estimate is built (§3.2). Any long-distance inquiry defaults to offering a walkthrough.
- Build the estimate. See §3.4.
- Review & send. Preview the client-facing estimate, then send it together with the moving-services contract. Both go out through SmartMoving — to the client's inbox and by text.
- Follow up. Two callbacks, then cold (§3.5).
- Book. On signature, hand to scheduling (§5).

| TASK GOVERNANCE Every active lead carries exactly one open task until the contract and estimate are both signed, the job is on the calendar, and nothing else is pending. Tasks live in SmartMoving, are linked to a customer and an opportunity, and follow the Action / Update / Context note format: the next action on top and revised every time; a dated, initialed update line per touch (Text, Management Email, Google Voice, or SM); and a static context blurb set once at creation. |
|---|

### 3.2 Walkthrough decision

A walkthrough is offered when any of the following apply: a large home (roughly 5,000+ sq ft or 5+ bedrooms), significant packing (full-house or the majority of rooms), or significant aggregate value (multiple antiques, an art collection, high-end furnishings throughout). Long-distance inventory accuracy matters more than local, so a walkthrough is the default offer on any LD inquiry.

Walkthrough inventory is captured in the Crew App, which populates a crew-log sheet in Drive. Whoever sits down to build the estimate pastes that inventory into the estimate engine; the norm is that the person who ran the walkthrough builds the estimate, since they carry the in-person read of the job on top of the item list.

### 3.3 The sales call

The sales script (V6.1) is the framework for how we aspire to run calls: a structured flow that qualifies the lead, branches on move type (local, long-distance, walkthrough) and on the optional move-out clean, gathers a room-by-room inventory when no walkthrough is warranted, sets honest expectations on the hourly model, and closes by asking permission to follow up. It is the reference for call technique; the pipeline steps above are the process it sits inside.

### 3.4 Building the estimate

Estimates are produced with a dedicated Claude project (the “estimate engine”) and finished by hand. The engine's full rate-and-formula prompt is maintained as a living artifact separate from this SOP; only the process is recorded here.

- Screenshot the client's name and contact details, and the origin and destination addresses, from the SmartMoving lead.
- Paste those, plus the call and/or web-form notes, into a new chat in the estimate-engine project. For a walkthrough job, paste the captured inventory. For a long-distance job, first call Penske for a one-way rental quote and feed it in — the engine cannot price the driving leg without it.
- The engine returns calculation notes followed by a client-facing draft. Review and adjust.
- Paste the finished estimate into the large text field of the SmartMoving estimate — that field is the estimate; the structured fields around it are redundant.

| HOW MUCH TO TRUST THE ENGINE Estimating still runs primarily on experienced judgment. The engine is a drafting starting point whose output is, at present, less trustworthy than crew intuition — it reliably estimates low on medium jobs, and its timing and total-cost figures are manually corrected upward before sending. Improving this is the object of the separate estimate-accuracy initiative. |
|---|

### 3.5 Follow-up & continuity

Follow-up runs on two callbacks: the first 24–48 hours after sending, the second 5–7 days out; if the second goes unanswered the lead is marked cold. Call, then voicemail, then text. The task's Action/Update/Context note is updated on every touch.

Continuity of ownership is preferred but not mandated. We make a reasonable effort to keep one salesperson as the client's single point of contact, which matters most on long-distance jobs, where the relationship often runs for weeks.

## 5 · Scheduling & Dispatch

### 5.1 Who schedules, and when

There is no fixed scheduling owner — in practice it is whoever gets to it first, and that is most often Jonas. He is the default by virtue of an intimate read on each crew member's capability and capacity, and when he has the bandwidth his habit is to schedule every job he can. The pipeline reinforces this: because assigning crew is the last step after booking, whoever books a job tends to staff it, which closes the loop and keeps the day's remaining availability legible — a booked job with unassigned crew muddies the view of what capacity is left.

### 5.2 Where the decision is made

Assignment is decided and recorded in Google Calendar. The working setup is two windows side by side: the Google Calendar weekly view, and the Crew App's admin availability display (Month Schedule View / Crew Resources). Crew submit their own availability in the Crew App — marking days red, green, or orange — but the assignment decision is still made against that view next to the calendar; the app is not yet the scheduling surface. The legacy Google availability sheet was hard-sunset and the Crew App is now the single source for availability.

### 5.3 Confirming crew

Crew are confirmed by accepting the Google Calendar invite. There is no cap on how long an invite can sit before acceptance; this is currently tolerable because Crew App availability is actively maintained, so invites go to people who have already signaled they are open, and a crew member who cannot work almost always reaches out proactively to arrange a substitution or update their availability.

### 5.4 Calendar mechanics

The Mountaineer calendar is the primary job calendar. SmartMoving auto-generates events for booked jobs, but it refreshes and overwrites edits, so a SmartMoving event is duplicated onto the Mountaineer calendar before it is edited. Each event follows a standardized crew-brief format (job and quote number, crew lead, dispatch and arrival times, client details, addresses and stops, crew responsibilities, job description, equipment, trucks and crew count, inventory, and billing summary). An auto-formatter that writes these clean briefs from the SmartMoving event is in testing, not live.

### 5.5 Assignment rules

- A dedicated crew lead on every job where possible; a sub-lead when not.
- New movers paired with a lead.
- Two leads on designer jobs.
- Hours prioritized to senior movers when work is thin.

### 5.6 M1 jobs

M1 pre-sells designer and install work and posts it to the shared calendar as pink “MM” banner events. M1's administrator (Devnee) owns scheduling and confirmation for these; Mountaineer verifies, duplicates the confirmed event onto the Mountaineer calendar, reformats it, and staffs it. We are sometimes looped into designer email threads about resource availability. The relationship terms are in §17.

## 6 · Field Operations

Field operations run in three layers: the job-day process and data capture, the crew lead's on-site authority, and the craft standards for the physical work. The first two are drawn from established company practice; the craft standards in §6.3 are general moving practice, held as a baseline pending the training-development effort with senior leads.

### 6.1 Job-day workflow & Crew App inputs

Every job is run and recorded through the Crew App It covers the full job-day lifecycle, works offline and syncs on reconnect, and stamps every event with GPS coordinates; all times are Mountain. What follows is what the crew inputs at each phase and why each input matters to the business. Step-by-step app mechanics live in the Crew Reference and Admin guides, which this SOP references rather than reproduces.

#### Before leaving the yard

- Pick the job while online. The crew selects the job from the calendar dropdown before leaving dispatch. This assigns a shared job ID, so multiple crew on the same event have their logs merged into one record automatically; a manual-entry fallback exists for no-signal situations and matches against names other crew have already typed.
- Pre-trip DVIR. A 25-item inspection before the truck moves. Any noted defect sends the DVIR to the mechanic-review queue and the truck is not to be driven until an admin clears it (§11).
- Interstate only — Prior On-Duty Hours Statement. Filed per driver before departure, declaring hours worked over the prior 7 days and 24 hours (§12).

#### On the road and on the job

- START at departure. Tapped when the crew pulls away from dispatch — billable time begins here.
- Arrive / Depart at every stop. Logged at origin, destination, storage, dump sites, and every intermediate stop — not just the main site. Admin reconciles hours and mileage from this data, so intermediate stops matter.
- Materials as used. Logged from the catalog or as custom items during the job; this feeds the bill.
- Photos. Captioned where relevant — fragile items, pre-existing damage, company-caused damage; stored per job in Drive and visible to all crew and admin on the job.
- Interstate only — RODS and the Digital Bill of Lading. Drivers log duty-status changes (Record of Duty Status) through the day. The Digital BOL is built in the field: inventory by item with condition notes and photos, each box marked Company Packed or Packed By Owner (which determines carrier liability for the contents), and signed by client and crew at origin before loading. See §12.

#### End of job

- FINISH on return. Tapped back at dispatch — billable time ends.
- Post-trip DVIR. Completed after the last job of the day.
- Job Report (crew lead). Captures the job's closing data: personal-vehicle count, dumpster and recycling percentages, billing method, whether to seek a client review, an hours-match confirmation (does the timeline reflect the hours billed and paid), per-person employee hours, and optional crew feedback to the office.
- Review and submit the Bill (crew lead). The bill auto-populates from timeline events, materials, and the dumpster/recycling sliders. The lead reviews line items, adds or edits custom charges, tips, and discounts, and checks the “Reviewed” box — which gates submission of the whole report.
- Interstate only — destination BOL signing. At delivery: the final walkthrough, a damage note (or NONE), any changed final charges, and a second client-and-crew signature. The signed PDF is handed to the client on the spot.

#### Crew inputs outside the job-day sequence

Two crew inputs run through the app independent of any single job:

- Expenses & reimbursement. Crew log mileage (odometer readings with start and end photos) and business expenses (amount, category, vendor, receipt photo). A personal-card purchase requests reimbursement; a company-card purchase is log-only. Submissions carry a status through admin review (submitted, approved, rejected) and write to a reimbursements sheet. This replaces texting receipts and odometer photos.
- Scheduling availability. Crew submit availability in rolling two-week windows, each day marked available, unavailable, or conditional. Once submitted a crew member is committed to days marked available unless scheduled with three or fewer days' notice; locked windows require an admin unlock to change. This is the availability the office schedules against (§5).
Corrections. Timeline events can be adjusted within a seven-day window; original timestamps are preserved. Billing runs portal to portal — from leaving dispatch to returning — rounded to the quarter hour after five minutes, with a two-hour minimum.

### 6.2 Crew lead authority

The crew lead owns execution on site. Their authority and its limits:

- May: direct and sequence the work, delegate by skill, communicate with the client, apply overtime and the senior/veteran 10% discount, bill to the contract and calendar, and stop work.
- Stop-work triggers: unsafe conditions, property at risk, missing equipment, weather or access problems, or a hostile client.
- May not: grant discounts beyond 10%, change rates, waive fees, make commitments outside the contract, or override calendar, valuation, or designer flags.
Leading the crew. The lead assigns roles on arrival (truck packer, disassembly lead, heavy-carry team, box team), sequences the job explicitly, and commands decisively rather than by committee on larger crews. Late arrivals more than five minutes out, when an insured driver is already present, are sent to meet the crew at origin. The lead coaches new or struggling movers through the work.

Client communication on site. Set scope and expectations at the start; send ETA texts when leaving dispatch or a waypoint on Big Sky, M1, and designer jobs; warn the client early if cost is trending past estimate; de-escalate conflict rather than argue it.

Escalation. Escalate early when money, legality, or the company's reputation is at stake — safety incidents, cost or scope disputes, major damage, a mismatch between the manifest and the goods, or a hostile client.

### 6.3 Craft standards

These are principle-level standards for the physical work, held as a general baseline. See SS 10 for further refinement through training development.

#### Jobsite order of operations

Every job follows the same underlying sequence, scaled to size, set by the lead on arrival: walk the home with the client first (confirm scope, identify fragile and high-value and specialty items, note pre-existing damage, map the path, confirm what the client is handling); protect the path before any load-out (floor runners, door-jamb and rail protection); stage boxes and small items near the exit in load order; disassemble anything safer or smaller in parts, bagging and labeling hardware to its piece; pad and wrap furniture in the room where it stands, not at the truck; load; drive; unload and place items in their final positions, blanket staying on until final placement; reassemble; and close with a final walkthrough with the client, at which any Mountaineer-attributable damage is identified and documented — the contract deems failure to record damage then as acceptance of condition.

#### Truck-loading method

Load heaviest items first, low and forward against the cab. Build in tiers floor to ceiling in tight walls so nothing shifts. Stand flat and vertical surfaces — dressers, bagged mattresses, padded tabletops, headboards — on edge along the walls. Load fragile and light items on top and last; they come off first. Load rugs last and roll them out first at destination. Tie off each tier as it is built, using the truck's rail points, rather than securing only at the end. Where possible, load in reverse unload order.

#### Packing technique by item class

Method is governed by what goes into the box, not the box: dishes and glassware in dish barrels, wrapped and cushioned top, bottom, and between layers; mirrors, glass, and framed art in mirror packs or crates, faces protected and never flat under weight; electronics in original boxes where possible, screens protected and cords bagged to the device; hanging clothes in wardrobe boxes; soft goods in large boxes, used to cushion but never as the sole protection for anything fragile; general and miscellaneous in medium boxes, with heavy items kept in small boxes so no box exceeds a safe carry weight. Every box is packed full and tight, labeled by room and contents, and sealed; fragile boxes are marked and loaded last. Contents of client-packed boxes are the client's liability.

#### Physical handling

Lift with the legs and within capacity; anything flagged as a two-person item is a team carry, and heavy or awkward items are dollied rather than muscled. Dollies are strapped and tilted under control with a spotter guarding the path, and a loaded dolly never takes stairs without a spotter below. Stairs are the highest-risk moment of any job — slow down,, put the strong mover on the downhill weight, keep a spotter below, and move one item at a time; the stair penalty in estimating exists because this work is deliberately slow and should not be rushed. Blanket-wrap furniture before it moves and leave the wrap on to final placement. Runners and jamb protection go in before load-out, not reactively. No heavy item moves silently — team lifts move on a spoken count, and the person walking backward has a spotter's eyes.

#### Hot tub moves

Current method carries known risk. We routinely move hot tubs as a specialty item. Present practice is to tip the tub onto a side panel and move it on multiple four-wheel dollies or on heavy industrial tarps and sliders. This is inherently dangerous and routinely causes minor scuffing, dings, cracks, or damage to the downward panel. Two efforts are underway to address it, both currently orphaned: tightening the discipline of issuing extensive disclaimers and caveats on every hot-tub move during the initial sales call to limit liability, and evaluating safer methods such as a purpose-built spa dolly. Until that work concludes, treat every hot-tub move as a flagged, disclaimer-first job.

## 7 · Billing, Collections & Payments

### 7.1 Billing rules

All client-facing rates and terms are set by the Contract for Moving Services, which is the authority in any dispute with another document. The core rules:

- Labor $80/mover/hr; truck $90/hr; packing $90/hr unless a truck is billed on the job, in which case packing bills at the $80 mover rate; crew transport vehicle $100/day.

From there the flow is manual:

- She enters it into the catch-all spreadsheet — currently the authoritative billing record.
- She builds each invoice by hand in QuickBooks from the catch-all. There is no automated flow between them.

| A SYSTEM IN TRANSITION The catch-all spreadsheet is on its way out — it is to be retired and replaced by the Crew App log — and Jacob intends to wire the Crew App directly into QuickBooks. Until that lands, the catch-all remains a single point of failure carrying billing, payroll, and onboarding data at once, entered and re-entered by hand. |

### 7.3 Payment terms & collections

Invoices carry 30-day terms in practice, and QuickBooks begins applying the late fee automatically after that. There is no formal collections cadence: around the end of the term Hailey both calls or texts a reminder and resends the invoice. Escalation is ad hoc — a principal has once or twice been asked to leave a voicemail raising the prospect of a collections agency, though whether an agency has ever actually been engaged is unclear.

Contract-vs-practice note. The contract states 14-day terms; practice runs 30. One or the other should be reconciled (see Appendix A).

### 7.4 On-site payment

Crews that collect payment at job completion deliver it in person to Hailey's desk. After hours or on weekends they leave it in a drop box on the warehouse exterior, which Hailey empties herself — checked roughly weekly, or whenever a crew Job Report notes that payment was left there. The Job Report mention is the trigger signal that a drop-box payment is waiting.

## 8 · Damage Claims

Damage handling works, but it runs entirely on communication and negotiation and lives in no system of record.

### 8.1 On-site capture

When damage is noticed or caused, crew are asked to do two things: notify the office immediately by text with a photo, and point the damage out to the client in a timely way (usually immediately, though not always the right moment). This sits alongside the contract's mandatory final walkthrough, at which any Mountaineer-attributable damage must be identified and documented — the contract's single strongest protection, since failure to record damage then is deemed acceptance of condition.

### 8.2 Intake & resolution

If a client wants repair, replacement, or reimbursement — whether raised on site or by contacting us after the job — the on-call representative reaches out when able to discuss liability and solutions, and negotiates, usually by phone. The standing practice is to debrief the crew first to understand what happened before approaching the client.

Residential. Most claims resolve as a negotiated fixed-dollar credit or a global percentage discount on the total cost of service. In practice this is a commercial negotiation rather than a valuation-coverage payout, even though the contract carries a valuation framework.

Designer installs (M1 path). Damage we are not liable for — faulty or defective inventory — is returned to M1, which returns it to the vendor for replacement. Damage we cause, we attempt to return to the warehouse for on-site repair by M1 Ian (M1-affiliated; not the Mountaineer crew lead Ian Lange).

### 8.3 The record gap

Damage claims exist nowhere but the client communication channel they arrived in. There is no log, tracker, case record, status, or outcome data; no link between a claim and the job that caused it; and no feedback into training or crew evaluation. The Crew App captures damage photos at the job, but that capture does not become a claim record. As a result we cannot see claim volume, cost, cause, or which jobs and crews generate them, and settlements are negotiated with no precedent record. Contract claims themselves must be submitted in writing to the management inbox within nine months of delivery or notice of loss.

## 9 · Payroll & HR

### 9.1 Hours & pay cycle

Crew log time through the Crew App job timeline; hours pull into the Crew App spreadsheet, the same data path that feeds invoicing. Payroll runs biweekly: the pay week is Monday through Sunday, and payroll is completed on the Monday for the period ending the preceding day. There is no separate payroll tool — Hailey pulls hours from the Crew App, enters them into the catch-all, and keys them by hand into QuickBooks. (Wiring the Crew App directly into QuickBooks is a planned build; the catch-all is being retired in favor of the Crew App log.)

### 9.2 Hours approval

Logged hours pay out as-is, provided there is no discrepancy between billable and logged hours. Non-billable work incurred during the day — for example moving blankets between trucks at day's end — is paid at the $22.50 non-billable rate. There is otherwise no hours audit and largely no mechanism for one; the Crew App's geo-tagged timestamps act as a passive deterrent that improves reporting fidelity by implication rather than by enforcement.

### 9.3 Overtime

Crew pay complies with Montana overtime law for hours over 40 in a work week. Long-distance drive time is exempt from that requirement. This is distinct from the contract's client-billing overtime rules in §7.

### 9.4 Onboarding & offboarding

Onboarding is delivered by a script living in the catch-all document that sends a preformatted email with links to the payroll-setup portal, the crew handbooks, and Crew App install and setup. There is no formal offboarding process.

### 9.5 Time off, discipline, workers' comp

Time off is not requested in advance; crew simply mark their availability in the Crew App. Discipline is rare and has no formal guidelines; a punctuality-tracking effort once existed and is now orphaned. Workers' compensation coverage is in place for all crew.

## 10 · Crew Training, Evaluation & Compensation

### 10.1 Tier progression

A crew member moves up a tier one of two ways: they initiate the conversation and we give feedback plus a timeline to hit for the increase, or we notice someone excelling and either raise their pay immediately or set a timeline to do so. The decision is a management judgment — largely Jonas's — with no formal evidence standard. The tier-slip system supporting this is active: slips live in the trucks and are brought in routinely.

### 10.2 Evaluation

Evaluation is essentially one-on-one conversations with Jonas. Bad-job debriefs are very rare, reserved for especially egregious behavior. There is no systematic review cycle, which concentrates quality judgment in a single person's read of who is good.

### 10.3 Training

Training today is on-the-job absorption from senior leads, supplemented by a generic onboarding video that is not enforced and by the handbooks this SOP now supersedes. There is no standardized instruction in the moving craft itself; the craft standards in §6.3 are a general baseline. Developing the company's training practice is a forward effort to be shaped in consultation with senior leads Ian Lange and Andrew.

### 10.4 The skill tracker (intended future state)

A crew skill tracker has been drafted — a rubric of competencies to be deployed into the Crew App with job close-out records and skill ratings. The binding design constraint is maintenance burden, not tracking ambition:

| DESIGN PRINCIPLE Any evaluation system that requires manual per-job upkeep will not survive contact with the business. The tracker as drafted is too granular; adherence to hand-updating every crew member's skill tree after every job would collapse over a medium-term horizon. The tracker must be a byproduct of capture that already happens — ideally skills auto-assigned from job tags — rather than a new administrative task. A streamlined in-app rating interface raises the ceiling on how many skills can be sustainably tracked. Delegating peer evaluation to crew leads is a workable alternative, but it pushes more administrative load onto leads through the Crew App and carries its own adoption risk. |

## 11 · Fleet Maintenance

### 11.1 The fleet

Four active vehicles: a 24′ Freightliner (crew cab), a 26′ Freightliner (standard cab), a 26′ International, and a 16′ Ford E350 box van. The owned trucks run intrastate under Montana's 26,001-lb threshold; the Ford is exempt from DVIR, DOT-marking, and hours-of-service requirements. All interstate long-distance work runs on Penske rentals, which carry full federal compliance duties. Full compliance detail is in §12.

### 11.2 DVIR & the out-of-service loop

A DVIR defect means the vehicle is not roadworthy. A crew member notifies the office by text immediately. The Crew App can send a remote signature request to a mechanic to certify roadworthiness — that feature is untested. In practice Hailey calls Yeti Diesel and schedules service, and a vehicle is assumed back in service on pickup from Yeti unless it fails a subsequent DVIR. The formal out-of-service gate the app supports is not the gate actually used; pickup is.

### 11.3 Preventive maintenance

The Fleet Maintenance Log in Drive, maintained by Hailey, tracks service history and computes preventive-maintenance due and overdue dates. The log is current but stale in parts — it still lists a Mitsubishi box truck no longer owned, and its dashboard has at least one unit flagged overdue. The gap is not the tracking but the response: there is no defined process for acting on a preventive-maintenance-due alert, so tracked-due service can sit. Yeti Diesel is the dominant service vendor; historical vendors include Hayden Truck Repair, Real Steel Diesel, Washington Auto Carriage, and Tire Rama.

### 11.4 Truck readiness

Crew clean out trucks at day's end. Hailey stocks trucks with materials as she is able; otherwise crew pull from the materials room. The standard truck loadout is defined in §13.

## 13 · Materials Inventory

### 13.1 Catalog & consumption

Materials and their prices are defined in the contract; anything not listed is sold at cost plus 10%. Crew log materials as used in the Crew App during the job, and that flows into the bill — materials are billed as used, never pre-charged.
